Four brands, one system. The hard part is deciding what stays separate.

A design practice, a publication, a school and a marketplace can share the work behind the scenes. Each still owes its audience a clear promise.

The day the logo stopped being the problem

Four sites sound like four chances to make something work. They are also four places where a good idea can disappear behind a vague homepage, an outdated link or a form nobody checked.

The instinct is to redesign everything together. A more useful question comes first: if someone arrives from a search result, can they tell whose problem this page solves and what they can do next?

Give every brand one job

Oleh Hebel & Co helps product teams turn a messy interface or an AI prototype into a clearer workflow. Its proof is the decision behind a design and the work a client can inspect. The next step is a project brief.

Interface Report publishes analysis of AI and digital products. Its proof is an argument readers can check against sources. The next step is a relevant article or, for a team with a project to pitch, a clearly marked sponsored offer.

Ship Index Grow teaches people to build, launch and grow digital products. The proof is a guide that helps someone finish an actual task. The next step is an exercise, a checklist or the program.

Ridne Store helps people discover local Ukrainian producers. Here trust lives in the details: who made the product, where it comes from and how a buyer can reach the producer. Good copy can make those details easier to find. Only reliable listings can supply them.

Share the work that people should never have to notice

Use one release routine: check the canonical URL, follow the internal links, open the page on a phone and try its main action. Record what actually happened. This can be the same every time, whether the action is reading a story or finding a seller.

Where two brands meet, give the reader a reason to cross. A portfolio case can point to the publication it documents. A school lesson can link to an analysis that explains the underlying decision. A marketplace listing should send a shopper to the producer or the next meaningful step.

What we share, and what each site owns

A useful division starts with visible content. The marketplace owns the seller profile and offer details. The portfolio owns the design story: why the seller page makes provenance easy to find, which choices were made, and what still needs validation. If these pages repeat the same copy, a reader who follows both links gains nothing. Give each page only the part of the story that helps its own audience make a decision.

Next, choose a person or team responsible for maintaining each fact. Someone who changes a marketplace listing should update the source record, not chase every mention across a portfolio, a school and a publication. A course lesson can quote a specific example and link to it. An article can explain the trade-offs. Both can refer back to the authoritative page when the practical details change.

A shared design rule can be simple. A card on any of the four sites should make its destination clear. But a project card might show a design decision, an article card might show the question it answers, and a producer card might show the town and the product. Start with a common rule for clarity, then let the information hierarchy change with the task.

This also makes success easier to interpret. A reader who spends time with a difficult analysis may have a good experience even if they do not start a project brief. A buyer who finds a producer quickly may spend very little time on the marketplace. Choose a measure that matches each job. A shared website checklist can catch broken links, while each brand keeps its own definition of a valuable visit.

A four-line exercise before the next launch

Write down the person you serve, the problem they bring, the evidence they can verify and the next action. Repeat for each site. If two sites have identical answers, decide where the information should live and what the other site adds.

The system earns its keep when a stranger can move from question to evidence to action on any of the four sites. The brands earn their keep when the answers are different enough that the stranger knows exactly where to go.
