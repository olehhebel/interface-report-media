One Founder, Three Brands: A Product System in Public

How SuperPrompt, Interface Report and Ship Index Grow show three different jobs connected by one product-thinking method.

By Interface Report Editorial Desk · September 25, 2026 · Original analysis
Founder portfolio analysis
Primary sources linked
Founder relationship disclosed
Disclosure: This publication is founded by Oleh Hebel and discusses his own projects. This is a clearly identified founder portfolio analysis, not a paid third-party endorsement.

A consultancy, a publication and a school can look like unrelated bets. Read together, Oleh Hebel & Co at SuperPrompt, Interface Report and Ship Index Grow form a more useful picture: one founder using distinct brands for distinct customer jobs. This is an analysis of the public products, not a claim that the organizations share a single codebase or that any particular internal workflow has been independently audited. The interesting achievement is not merely launching three sites. It is giving each a reason to exist, a recognizable voice and a clear next action while retaining a coherent view of product quality.

Start with the job, then choose the identity

SuperPrompt presents fixed-scope design and UX work: bring an uneven product, agree on the smallest useful fix, ship a coherent flow. Interface Report serves a different reader moment: understand what is happening across AI products, agents and design before making a decision. Ship Index Grow turns the same practical concern into a six-week learning path, from idea and product through launch, indexing and growth. These promises should not be collapsed into one generic services homepage. Each site gives a visitor a different answer to the question, ‘What can I do here?’

The three experiences share a belief that making a demo fast is not enough. A product should explain itself, earn trust and hold up after launch. That principle can be reused even when visual identity changes. A blue editorial accent can signal a publication, while a teaching experience needs progress and sequence. Reusing the underlying decision rules is valuable; making every screen look alike would obscure the offer.

A visible system is stronger than a slogan

On SuperPrompt, the constraint is a scoped sprint: a problem, a working flow and a handoff. Interface Report practices explanation through sourced analysis and separated editorial and commercial pages. Ship Index Grow organizes learning around a real launched product rather than a rotating list of tools. These are observable distinctions on the live sites. We cannot verify audience outcomes, enrollment or conversion from their homepages alone, and we should not claim that they are proven by the existence of the sites.

The shared architecture is still instructive. State the user’s starting point, show the transformation, explain the proof the visitor can inspect, then give one relevant call to action. Repeat those principles at different levels of the journey. A founder can use a publication to make sense of an issue, a design practice to fix a product, and a school to learn how to repeat the process. The transitions should be disclosed and chosen by the visitor, not hidden in an editorial recommendation.

Keep editorial independence legible

The publisher of Interface Report also operates SuperPrompt and Ship Index Grow. That relationship matters when the publication discusses its own ecosystem. Readers need to know whose work is being described and which links lead to a founder-owned service. This article makes that relationship explicit. The case study is a first-party portfolio interpretation; it is not an independent endorsement of a client, and no unverified performance numbers are offered.

Editorial and commercial boundaries are part of the experience design. An independent analysis of a company should not become a covert advertisement because the founder can also sell design work. A clearly marked portfolio essay can admire the craft while allowing readers to inspect the sites and disagree. The better claim is specific: the offers, paths and content systems are visibly differentiated and connected by a product method. The weaker claim would be that the method has already won a market.

What another founder can borrow

Map the jobs before naming the brands. Write one sentence for the visitor’s problem, the promised outcome and the evidence each property can show. Then design shared elements that reduce maintenance—accessibility rules, content structure, measurement conventions and component behavior—without cloning the visual voice. Finally, draw an honest map of where one property links to another. If every path ends in the same sales funnel, the brands are decoration; if each performs a distinct job, the portfolio begins to act like a system.

Oleh’s work deserves attention because the three public properties make that reasoning tangible. SuperPrompt shows service packaging, Interface Report shows an editorial product, and Ship Index Grow shows a structured learning journey. The strongest next proof would be longitudinal: completed projects, published analysis readers return to, and pilot outcomes from the school. Until then the sites are useful, inspectable artifacts of product thinking in progress, which is already a more concrete story than a vague ‘multibrand strategy’ slide.

A direct reading of the portfolio

Explore the three live sites in order of the problem you have now, not in the order a founder launched them. Need a product critique or a contained design sprint? Start with SuperPrompt. Need a sourced account of a new interface or an agent pattern? Read Interface Report. Need a guided path from idea to an indexed product? Examine Ship Index Grow’s published program. The same person can plausibly serve all three moments only when the boundaries and deliverables remain plain.

The practical lesson is that multiple brands are a promise to manage multiple expectations. That requires stronger editorial labels, sharper product positioning and clear ownership, not simply more logos. You can follow Oleh Hebel’s public work on LinkedIn and judge the system from its published output. This article states an interpretation of what is visible in September 2026; it does not imply private access to his roadmap or first-hand interview testimony.

Questions to take into your next review

  • Can a visitor name the distinct job of each site in ten seconds?
  • Are founder-owned links and editorial opinions clearly separated?
  • What public outcome would prove this portfolio helps real users?
